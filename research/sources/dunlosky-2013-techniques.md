# Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques (Dunlosky et al. 2013, PSPI)

Why: retrieval practice (testing effect) and spacing for a Learn page players return to;
which techniques have the broadest evidence?

## Assessment

- Evidence: A. Psychological Science in the Public Interest monograph, five authors,
  very widely cited; rates each technique across learners, materials, tasks and
  classroom evidence.
- Reasoning: fair here, since only the summary was readable; the full reviews give the
  reasons.
- Interests: none known.
- Weigh: trust the ranking; effects are mostly on memory and comprehension of school
  material, not strategy games.

## Findings

- [learning] Practice testing and spaced practice rated high utility: they work across ages, abilities, tasks and real classrooms. ^f1
  > Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility assessments because they benefit learners of different ages and abilities and have been shown to boost students’ performance across many criterion tasks and even in educational contexts.
- [learning] Self-explanation, elaborative interrogation and interleaving rated moderate: promising, thinner evidence. ^f2
  > Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice received moderate utility assessments.
- [learning] Rereading and highlighting rated low; replace rereading with testing. ^f3
  > these techniques do not consistently boost students’ performance, so other techniques should be used in their place (e.g., practice testing instead of rereading).
- [learning] (inferred) For Learn: an idea a player discovered should come back later as a fresh draft quiz (spaced retrieval), mixed with other ideas (interleaved), rather than as a page to reread; asking "why did that win?" is self-explanation. ^f4
