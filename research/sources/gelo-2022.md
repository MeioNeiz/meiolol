# Wang 2023: Graph Embedding Augmented Skill Rating System (GElo, IEEE ToG)

Why: (c) does a player graph (who beat whom) improve skill ratings, and does it help
players with little history?

## Assessment

- Evidence: C. Peer-reviewed (IEEE ToG) but one author, no code link, only baseline is
  vanilla Elo (no Glicko, TrueSkill or logistic model), tiny professional datasets (531
  to 1,158 players, 606 to 8,393 matches).
- Reasoning: fair. Rolling time split with paired t-tests and CIs over 10 windows is
  sound; it reports its own null on LoL and on new players.
- Interests: author's method.
- Weigh: the gain is over a weak baseline whose known flaw (slow convergence for
  inactive players) the method patches; read it as "graph embeddings fix Elo's lag",
  not "graphs beat good ratings". Nothing for cold-start players: new ones get the mean.

## Findings

- [method] Graph: players are nodes, an edge joins two who met, weighted by the
  win-loss gap between them; embeddings come from DeepWalk-style random walks.
  Section III-B ^f1
  > We build a skill gap graph G = (V, E) based on player match histories, where G is an undirected weighted graph.
  > The player embeddings are learned from the graph using a random walk-based graph embedding method
- [method] The embeddings only adjust Elo after the fact: active players (above an
  elbow in games played) get bonus points scaled by cosine similarity to the top
  player's embedding. Section III-D ^f2
  > Low-rated active players may have better skills than high-rated inactive players, so these low-rated active players will be granted bonus points upon their rating scores to set their positions right.
- [method] Time split: 13 time units in date order, rate on two units, score the next
  two, 10 rolling windows, 5 runs each; metric is the share of matches the
  higher-rated side loses. Section IV-B ^f3
  > The datasets are sorted by date in ascending order.
  > we use both Elo and GElo to calculate their respective rating scores of players from the match records in [t1 , t2 ], and examine the prediction error rate in [t3 , t4 ] using Elo.
- [method] Cold start is not addressed: players new in the test window get the
  average rating, as in Elo. Section IV-B ^f4
  > For new players added in [t3 , t4 ], we set their default rating scores as the average final score of all players from [t1 , t2 ].
- [method] Table I mean error, Elo vs GElo: SC2 0.2634 vs 0.2544, ATP 0.3319 vs
  0.3263, CS 1 0.3754 vs 0.3627, CS 2 0.3929 vs 0.3774 (all significant); LoL pro
  0.3794 vs 0.3803, not significant. ^f5
  > GElo only gets a worse result in LOL dataset, but the difference is not significant.
- [method] The gain over Elo shrinks as the share of new players grows (slope -0.027,
  p = 0.082). Section IV-C ^f6
  > As the proportion of new players increases, the performance change of GElo against Elo reduces.
- [data] Datasets are small and professional: SC2 8,393 matches, ATP 6,273, CS 1
  606, CS 2 1,049, LoL 1,713 (pro championship). Table III ^f7
  > It includes professional championship matches.
- [method] Checked, not there: no baseline beyond vanilla Elo (no Glicko, TrueSkill,
  logistic or Bradley-Terry fit on all games), no team-level model (5v5 games still
  rate by Elo on players), no log loss. ^f8
- [method] (inferred) For us: a who-beat-whom player graph mostly corrects a rating
  system that updates too slowly; our logistic player terms fit all games jointly, which
  already lets indirect opponents inform each other, so expect little. And it gives
  nothing to the 74% of players with no earlier game on their champion. ^f9
