# Belia, Fidler, Williams, Cumming 2005: Researchers misunderstand confidence intervals and standard error bars

Why: what do readers do wrong with hard range bars, even trained ones?

## Assessment

- Evidence: A. Psychological Methods, 473 respondents who were authors of journal
  articles in psychology, behavioural neuroscience and medicine; Cumming is the
  reference author on CIs; the canonical citation for error bar misreading (cited by
  Correll and Gleicher 2014 and Krzywinski and Altman 2013).
- Reasoning: fair from the abstract alone; method and numbers not read.
- Interests: the authors argue for CIs over p-values ("new statistics").
- Weigh: experts, not lay readers; if they fail, lay readers will do no better. The 22%
  within a factor of 2 figure is in [[krzywinski-altman-error-bars#^f1]], which reports
  this study.

## Findings

- [ux/uncertainty] Task: researchers moved two means with error bars until they judged
  them just significantly different (p < .05). Abstract ^f1
  > adjusted a figure until they judged 2 means, with error bars, to be just statistically significantly different
- [ux/uncertainty] Many leading researchers misread how error bars relate to
  significance, and do not tell CI bars from SE bars. Abstract ^f2
  > Results from 473 respondents suggest that many leading researchers have severe misconceptions about how error bars relate to statistical significance, do not adequately distinguish CIs and SE bars
- [ux/charts] The authors call for less ambiguous graphical conventions before CIs can
  help communication. Abstract ^f3
  > Better guidelines for researchers and less ambiguous graphical conventions are needed before the advantages of CIs for research communication can be realized.
- [ux/uncertainty] (inferred) A bar's length means different things (SE, 95% CI, 80%
  range) and even experts cannot read overlap as "A really beats B"; meio.lol should not
  expect readers to infer comparisons from overlapping range bars. ^f4
