# Predicting as a learning strategy (Brod 2021, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review)

Why: predict then reveal is the core loop planned for Learn; does predicting help, and
what makes it work?

## Assessment

- Evidence: B. Peer-reviewed, open-access review by a researcher who runs the key
  experiments (pupil-dilation studies); rests on lab tasks (trivia, geography), a few
  studies per point.
- Reasoning: strong. Separates mechanisms, says where evidence is thin and that
  predicting vs guessing is a difference of degree.
- Interests: author's own studies feature heavily.
- Weigh: trust the design rule (commit with some confidence, then immediate feedback);
  the surprise mechanism is "initial evidence".

## Findings

- [learning] Predicting before the answer is a long-used teaching strategy (predict, observe, explain in science). ^f1
  > Asking students to generate a prediction before presenting the correct answer is a popular instructional strategy.
- [learning] Predicting needs some confidence and immediate feedback; that is what separates it from guessing. ^f2
  > Predicting involves that learners generate a response with at least some confidence, followed by immediate feedback.
- [learning] Wrong predictions cause surprise, which raises attention to the correct answer and helps it stick. ^f3
  > Initial evidence suggests that predicting boosts surprise about unexpected answers, which leads to enhanced attention to the correct answer and strengthens its encoding.
- [learning] The order matters: stating the belief after seeing the answer (postdiction) gave no surprise response. ^f4
  > There was no difference in pupil dilation in the postdiction condition.
- [learning] Not any activity before the answer works: generating examples did not cause the surprise response, predicting did. ^f5
  > A follow-up study confirmed that it is not the case that any generative activity before presentation of the correct answer suffices to induce surprise
- [learning] Low-confidence guesses barely surprise, so predicting with no basis (pure guessing) loses the effect. ^f6
  > This indicates that surprise cannot be a major driving force underlying the effectiveness of guessing.
- [learning] Confident wrong answers are the most likely to be corrected (hypercorrection). ^f7
  > The hypercorrection effect refers to the finding that erroneous responses committed with high confidence are more likely to be corrected than erroneous responses committed with low confidence
- [learning] Predicting raises curiosity more than generating examples. ^f8
  > It was found that generating predictions led to higher curiosity ratings than generating examples, indicating that generating predictions indeed triggers curiosity.
- [learning] Practice testing (retrieval) beats restudying, and testing with feedback beats testing alone. ^f9
  > Practice testing with feedback has been shown to be more effective than testing alone
- [learning] (inferred) For Learn: ask for a pick or a "who wins" call with an optional confidence before showing the win rate; reveal straight away; draft situations where the obvious answer is wrong (expectancy violation) teach most. Asking players to guess about champions they don't know is guessing, not predicting. ^f10
