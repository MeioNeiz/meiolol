# Stephen Few: Practical Rules for Using Color in Charts (2008)

Why: the canonical practitioner rules on limiting colour in data displays.

## Assessment

- Evidence: A. Few is a recognised expert on dashboard and table design (Information
  Dashboard Design, Show Me the Numbers); this newsletter is widely cited.
- Reasoning: strong. Each rule shown with a failing and a fixed example, grounded in
  perception (contrast is relative).
- Interests: sells books and courses.
- Weigh: written for light backgrounds ("use white"); on dark, the rule is a uniform
  background, not white. Rule 8 suggests blue and red for heat maps, our scale is blue and
  orange.

## Findings

- [ux/colour] Colour is for a communication goal, never decoration. Rule 3 ^f1
  > Use color only when needed to serve a particular communication goal.
  > Don’t use color to decorate the display.
- [ux/colour] Different colours only for different meanings. Rule 4 ^f2
  > Use different colors only when they correspond to differences of meaning in the data.
- [ux/colour] [ux/density] Soft natural colours for most data; bright or dark only to highlight what needs attention. Rule 5 ^f3
  > Use soft, natural colors to display most information and bright and/or dark colors to highlight information that requires greater attention.
- [ux/colour] Experts restrain colour: the belief that more and brighter is better is wrong. ^f4
  > Regarding color, people tend to think that more and brighter is better. To illustrate the point that the opposite is true
- [ux/colour] Sequential values: one hue (or a few related ones), pale to intense. Rule 6 ^f5
  > stick with a single hue (or a small set of closely related hues) and vary intensity from pale colors for low values to increasingly darker and brighter colors for high values.
- [ux/colour] [ux/density] Non-data elements (gridlines, borders, fills) just visible enough, no more. Rule 7 ^f6
  > Non-data components of tables and graphs should be displayed just visibly enough to perform their role, but no more so, for excessive salience could cause them to distract attention from the data.
- [ux/colour] A cell's colour looks different on different backgrounds: keep the background uniform where colours must compare. Rule 1 ^f7
  > If you want different objects of the same color in a table or graph to look the same, make sure that the background—the color that surrounds them—is consistent.
- [ux/colour] No red with green; blue and red for positive and negative in a heat map. Rule 8 ^f8
  > A good substitute in a heatmap for distinguishing positive and negative values is blue and red, rather than green and red.
- [ux/colour] Categorical colours should be equally intense so none pops out; his sample palette has eight hues. ^f9
  > They should look different without varying in perceived intensity.
  > Here’s a palette of eight sample hues that fall in the medium range of intensity
