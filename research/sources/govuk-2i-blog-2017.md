# Inside GOV.UK: Talk more, queue less (2017)

Why: what a mature content team keeps as human review, beside its numeric style rules.

## Assessment

- Evidence: B. First-hand report by the GOV.UK content team on its own process; no
  numbers on turnaround.
- Reasoning: fair. Says why 2i exists and what went wrong (queue).
- Interests: none.
- Weigh: process is from 2017; GOV.UK relaunched its guidance in June 2026 (linked in
  the post's sidebar), 2i itself still appears there.

## Findings

- [web/qa] Every new piece of GOV.UK content goes through 2i: someone else reviews it and
  authors cannot publish their own work. Intro ^f1
  > Every time we publish new content on GOV.UK, it goes through what we call ‘2i’ - short for ‘second pair of eyes’. Someone else has to look at what you’ve done and you can’t hit the ‘publish’ button yourself.
- [web/qa] Why human: authors lose the ability to read their own text as a first-time
  user. Intro ^f2
  > when you spend time writing a piece of content it becomes harder to imagine yourself as a user reading it for the first time.
- [web/qa] The bottleneck was reviewers: far more writers than trained 2i reviewers, so
  changes queued. Intro ^f3
  > we ended up with far more people producing content than we had people trained to 2i it.
- [web/qa] Fix: talk through the plan and the draft with a peer first, so 2i passes first
  time more often. There's now a series of chats ^f4
  > because the quality is now much higher, more content flies straight through 2i and is published first time round.
- [web/qa] (inferred) Relevant to many agents adding UI: a final reviewer who did not
  write the page (an agent with a rubric and screenshots) is GOV.UK's model; the
  bottleneck lesson says to keep the rubric short and catch most issues earlier by lint.
  ^f5
