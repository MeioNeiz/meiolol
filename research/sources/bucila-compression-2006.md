# Bucila, Caruana, Niculescu-Mizil 2006: Model Compression

Why: the origin of mimic training. How much of an ensemble does a small student keep,
and does labelling extra (synthetic) inputs with the teacher matter?

## Assessment

- Evidence: A as the canonical origin of model compression (KDD, heavily cited, the
  basis of Ba-Caruana and Hinton). The experiments are B: eight binary UCI-style
  problems, 4000 training rows each, RMSE on large test sets, no error bars.
- Reasoning: fair. Clear ablation of pseudo-data generators (RANDOM, NBE, MUNGE) and
  of pseudo-data size; the one failure (ADULT) is reported with possible reasons.
- Interests: authors' own ensemble selection method is the teacher.
- Weigh: trust the mechanism (teacher-labelled extra inputs let a student fit the
  teacher without overfitting) and that synthetic inputs must stay on the data
  manifold. Discount the 97% figure for us: student is a net, not an additive model, and
  4k labelled rows is far smaller than our 230k.

## Findings

- [method] Core idea: label a large unlabelled or synthetic set with the teacher and
  train the small model on it; with enough pseudo data the student cannot overfit. ^f1
  > A fast, compact and expressive model trained on enough pseudo data will not overfit and will approximate well the function learned by the high performing model.
- [method] Synthetic inputs must match the real joint distribution: independent
  per-attribute sampling (RANDOM) covers useless regions and stops helping, even hurts,
  past 100k pseudo rows. ^f2
  > For mimic nets trained with pseudo data generated by RANDOM, performance improves slightly at 10k, and at 100k and beyond performs worse than a neural net trained on just the original 4k train set.
- [method] MUNGE: for each example find its nearest neighbour and, with probability
  p, swap each nominal attribute between them (continuous ones are jittered toward
  each other); repeat k times. Algorithm 1 ^f3
  > Given example e and its closest other example e0 , the values for each noncontinuous attribute are swapped between e and e0 with probability p and are left unchanged with probability 1 − p.
- [method] [stats] With 400k MUNGE rows and 256 hidden units, the mimic nets kept on
  average 97% of the gain the ensemble had over a net trained on the original 4k
  rows. Table 2 ^f4
  > On average, model compression with MUNGE is able to achieve 97% of the performance increase that could at best be expected.
- [method] The one failure was the dataset with high-arity nominal attributes
  (ADULT: 14, 16, 41 values, 71 sparse binary inputs): the mimic net was barely better
  than a net on the original data. ^f5
  > Interestingly, ADULT is the only data set that has high-arity nominal attributes.
- [method] Real unlabelled data beats synthetic data when available. Section 6 ^f6
  > MUNGE, however, is not as effective as drawing unlabeled data from the true distribution, so using true unlabeled data is preferred when it is available.
- [method] The student can beat its teacher where the teacher overfit (HS problem):
  the student acts as a smoother. ^f7
  > We suspect that the ensemble has overfit the data, and that the neural net may provide a beneficial form of smoothing/regularization.
- [method] (inferred) Our inputs are exactly the failure case of ^f5: high-arity
  nominal (170 champions x 5 roles x 2 sides). The fix for us is not MUNGE on rows
  but synthetic drafts: swapping one champion between real drafts (MUNGE's nominal
  swap) gives the teacher's view on rare ally and enemy pairs that 230k games barely
  cover, and the teacher labels them for free. ^f8
- [method] (inferred) Keep synthetic drafts on the manifold (^f2): swap within a role
  and keep picks that occur in real games; uniform random drafts would mostly teach
  the student about compositions nobody plays. ^f9
