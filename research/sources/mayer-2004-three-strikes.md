# Should There Be a Three-Strikes Rule Against Pure Discovery Learning? (Mayer 2004, American Psychologist)

Why: "let players figure it out" risks pure discovery; what does the evidence say about
how much guidance a discovery page needs? (Host site is down; read via the Wayback copy.)

## Assessment

- Evidence: A. Widely cited American Psychologist review by a leading learning
  scientist; rests on many studies across 40 years, mostly older lab and classroom
  work.
- Reasoning: strong. Explains the mechanism (select relevant information, integrate
  it) and separates constructivism as theory from pure discovery as method.
- Interests: none beyond the author's own cognitive theory of learning.
- Weigh: trust the direction (guided over pure); it gives no recipe for how much
  guidance, as it says itself.

## Findings

- [learning] In all three research lines, guided discovery beat pure discovery for learning and transfer. ^f1
  > In each case, guided discovery was more effective than pure discovery in helping students learn and transfer.
- [learning] Hints about what to attend to, without giving the rule, already helped: faster learning, more remembered. ^f2
  > The guided discovery group learned more efficiently than, remembered more than, and transferred to new problems just as well as the pure discovery group.
- [learning] With a hint about the general rule, guided discovery beat both no hints and being told the rule and answer. ^f3
  > The pure discovery group performed the worst and the guided discovery group performed the best on tests of immediate retention, delayed retention, and transfer to solving new problems.
- [learning] Guided discovery took longest but transferred best. ^f4
  > Although guided discovery required the most learning time, it resulted in the best performance on solving transfer problems.
- [learning] Pure discovery fails when the learner never meets the principle; exposition fails when it stops them making sense actively. ^f5
  > Pure discovery can be ineffective when it fails to promote the second criterion—that is, students may not come into contact with the to-be-learned principle
- [learning] Design rule: enough freedom to think, enough guidance to land on the right knowledge. ^f6
  > Students need enough freedom to become cognitively active in the process of sense making, and students need enough guidance so that their cognitive activity results in the construction of useful knowledge.
- [learning] Even minimal guidance, plain right or wrong feedback, was enough in the conservation studies. ^f7
  > This minimal level of guidance was sufficient to help students learn to give correct answers on subsequent conservation problems and be able to provide sound explanations for their answers.
- [learning] Activity is not learning: what matters is cognitive, not behavioural, activity. ^f8
  > the formula constructivism ⫽ hands-on activity is a formula for educational disaster.
- [learning] Open problem it names: how much and what kind of guidance, and stating the intended outcome. ^f9
  > The challenge of teaching by guided discovery is to know how much and what kind of guidance to provide and to know how to specify the desired outcome of learning.
- [learning] (inferred) For Learn: clicking through drafts without a stated target idea is pure discovery. Each exercise needs a named outcome, a hint that points attention ("look at the enemy's engage") without the answer, and right or wrong feedback straight after. ^f10
