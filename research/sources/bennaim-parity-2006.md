# Ben-Naim, Vazquez, Redner 2006: Parity and predictability of competitions

Why: reference upset rates for sports, to place a 54.8% (45.2% upset) draft model.

## Assessment

- Evidence: A. Over a century of five leagues, 300k games; well cited physics of sport
  paper.
- Reasoning: fair. Upset defined by the better record on the day, not a fitted model.
- Interests: none known.
- Weigh: their "favourite" is a crude predictor, so q is an upper bound on each
  sport's Bayes error, not an estimate.

## Findings

- [method/ceiling] Measured upset frequency (team with the worse record on the date
  wins): FA soccer 0.452, MLB 0.441, NHL 0.414, NBA 0.365, NFL 0.364. Table I ^f1
  > Our main result is that soccer and baseball are the most competitive sports with q = 0.452 and q = 0.441
- [method/ceiling] Spread of season win fractions: MLB sd 0.084 (typically .400 to
  .600), NFL 0.210. Sec. I ^f2
  > in baseball, where the winning fraction x typically falls between 0.400 and 0.600, the variance is σ = 0.084.
- [method/ceiling] A one-parameter model (favourite wins with 1 - q) ties the spread of
  standings to the upset rate, so predictability can be read off standings. Abstract ^f3
  > This model quantitatively relates the parity among teams with the predictability of the games
- [method/ceiling] With infinite games the spread is set by the upset probability alone:
  bias 1/2 - q and sd are equivalent measures. Sec. III ^f4
  > in a hypothetical season with an infinite number of games, the distribution is governed by the upset probability alone.
- [method/ceiling] (inferred) Our draft model's 45.2% error is at soccer and baseball
  levels with a crude favourite; ranked solo queue with MMR balancing should sit at or
  above those (more random), which makes a draft-only ceiling far above 56-57% hard to
  believe. ^f5
