# Measuring Learning Progress via Self-Explanations versus Problem Solving (Otieno et al. 2011, CogSci)

Why: worked examples and faded hints as the counterweight to discovery; when should a
Learn page show a solved draft, and when take the support away?

## Assessment

- Evidence: B. Peer-reviewed conference paper by the main worked-example and tutoring
  researchers; its intro summarises their earlier lab and field experiments (Salden et
  al. 2009, d about 0.5 to 0.6); the new result is correlational, one sample of
  geometry students.
- Reasoning: fair. States limits (one delayed near transfer test failed the hypothesis).
- Interests: the authors' own Cognitive Tutor work.
- Weigh: trust the summary of the fading literature; the self-explanation-as-signal
  result is one study.

## Findings

- [learning] Adding worked examples and self-explanation prompts to tutored problem solving improves learning. ^f1
  > be even more effective when worked examples are added in comparison to problem solving alone. Introducing selfexplanation prompts additionally improves learning.
- [learning] Worked examples help only early; for more expert learners they stop helping or hurt (expertise reversal). ^f2
  > As expertise increases, worked examples not only lose their effectiveness but can even impede learning
- [learning] Fading: steps of a worked example are handed to the learner one by one until they solve alone (Renkl and Atkinson 2003). ^f3
  > proposed a fading procedure in which problem-solving elements are successively integrated into example study until the students are able to solve problems on their own
- [learning] Adaptive fading, by the learner's performance, beat fixed fading, which beat plain problem solving. ^f4
  > found a monotonic trend of adaptive fading over fixed fading over problem solving.
- [learning] Adaptive fading's edge over the rest: d 0.49 immediate, 0.59 delayed. ^f5
  > revealed a significant superiority of the adaptive-fading condition in both immediate posttest (t(54) = 1.74, p < .05, d = .49) and delayed posttest (t(49) = 2.04, p < .05, d = .59)
- [learning] Whether a learner can give the reason predicts learning better than whether they get the answer. ^f6
  > Learning outcomes were better predicted by performance on reason steps (i.e., selfexplanation) than by performance on answer steps (i.e., problem solving).
- [learning] Some got answers right without being able to explain them. ^f7
  > Some students were able to write down mathematical values but failed to provide correct self-explanations.
- [learning] (inferred) For Learn: novices may need one solved draft first (why this pick won), then drafts where they make more of the calls, adapting to how they do; a "why?" step (choose the reason) is a better check of understanding than the pick alone. ^f8
