# When Problem Solving Followed by Instruction Works: Evidence for Productive Failure (Sinha and Kapur 2021)

Why: does letting a player try a draft problem before being told the idea beat explaining
first, and under what design conditions?

## Assessment

- Evidence: A. Peer-reviewed meta-analysis in a top review journal (RER 91(5)), 53
  studies, 166 comparisons, three-level model, publication-bias correction.
- Reasoning: strong. States the instruction-first case fairly, codes design fidelity,
  reports where PS-I loses (young learners, procedural and domain-general skills).
- Interests: Kapur coined productive failure; authors are its proponents.
- Weigh: trust the direction and boundary conditions; the bias-corrected 0.87 is a model
  estimate, the observed 0.36 is the safer number. Studies are mostly school maths and
  science, not games.

## Findings

- [learning] Problem solving before instruction beats instruction first for conceptual knowledge and transfer: moderate effect, g 0.36. ^f1
  > Our results showed a significant, moderate effect in favor of PS-I (Hedge’s g 0.36 [95% confidence interval 0.20; 0.51])
- [learning] Effects are larger when the design follows productive failure principles closely. ^f2
  > The effects were even stronger (Hedge’s g ranging between 0.37 and 0.58) when PS-I was implemented with high fidelity to the principles of Productive Failure (PF)
- [learning] No benefit for procedural knowledge; it helps understanding and transfer, not drills. ^f3
  > a nonsignificant effect (Hedge’s g) of −0.03, 95% CI [−0.20, 0.15] for procedural knowledge
- [learning] Boundary: for young learners and domain-general skills instruction first won. ^f4
  > Contrasting trends were, however, observed for younger age learners (second to fifth graders) and for the learning of domain-general skills, for which effect sizes favored I-PS.
- [learning] The failure phase must be followed by instruction that builds on the learner's own attempts, compared against the canonical solution. ^f5
  > Instruction that builds on student-generated solutions is more likely to make students aware of specific gaps in their reasoning
- [learning] The canonical idea is named after the attempt, by contrast with what the learner tried. ^f6
  > By explaining why those gaps exist (via comparison and contrast with the canonical solution)
- [learning] Generating several (suboptimal) solutions is the mechanism: it activates prior knowledge. ^f7
  > by generating multiple suboptimal solutions
- [learning] [fun] Problems should have affective draw: contrasting cases, authentic storylines, interactive settings. ^f8
  > It can be instantiated by creating intuitive hooks that engage students in design via contrasting cases, authentic storylines, and situating the problem within interactive learning environments
- [learning] The instruction-first case: novices flounder and overload working memory in trial and error. ^f9
  > Lack of prior knowledge can lead to time-consuming search through the solution space when students attempt to engage in sensemaking via trial and error, thereby burdening the limited capacity of the working memory.
- [learning] (inferred) For Learn: pose a real draft situation, let the player pick and explain, then reveal the data and name the idea against their own answer. Pure trial and error without the reveal is not what was tested. ^f10
