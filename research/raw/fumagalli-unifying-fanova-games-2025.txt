B     EXTENSIONS BEYOND OUR FRAMEWORK

In this section, we additionally provide some details on a broader perspective of the framework.

B.1    Beyond b-, m- and c-fANOVA

In our framework, we introduced three types of fANOVA decompositions, which correspond to the most promi-
nent examples used in XAI literature. The decomposition is specified by the choice of imputation method in
the value function FS . Less prominent examples include re-training of models (Strumbelj et al., 2009), where
for a subset of features S ⊆ D the model is fit to the data using only the features in S. While b-, m-, and
c-fANOVA compress the feature distribution information in the explanation, model re-training additionally in-
cludes the model fitting procedure. In future work, it would be interesting to extend our theoretical analysis to
such extensions for simple examples, like the linear regression model.

B.2    From Decompositions to Influence Measures and Back

Using a suitable type of imputation method, and thereby definition of FS , we obtain a fANOVA decomposition.
This is ensured by the recursive definition of fANOVA effects from Eq. (1). In fact, for the last effect, we have
                                                            X
                                           fD (x) = F (x) −     fS (x),
                                                           S⊂D

which yields a decomposition, as described for instance by Apley and Zhu (2020); Iwasawa and Matsumori (2024).
Moreover, this recursive definition related to the MT also ensures the orthogonality-like property (Apley and
Zhu, 2020; Iwasawa and Matsumori, 2024). Notably, partial individual, joint and interaction effects summarize
this fANOVA again into a functional decomposition (Bordt and von Luxburg, 2023) with components up to
order k, i.e.

In this context, the explanation ϕk up to order k can be viewed as a restricted fANOVA with components up
to order k. In future work, it would be interesting to understand how well such a low-complexity decomposition
describes the model.

F.1    Synthetic Examples

In this section, we describe in more detail the data settings and modeling approach of the synthetic example in
Section 5.1 and of further examples analyzed in more detail in Appendix G.1.

Datasets. For all our synthetic examples, we consider 4 features that are multivariate normally distributed,
                                   2
i.e., X ∼ N (µ, Σ) with µ = 0, σii   = 1 and σij = ρ for i ̸= j. We vary the correlation coefficient between
no, medium, and high correlation by setting it to ρ ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.9}. For these three data settings, we define the
following functional relationships

                                         Flin (x) = 2x1 + 2x2 + 2x3 ,
                                         Fint (x) = Flin + x1 x2 + x1 x2 x3 ,
                                        Fadd (x) = 2x1 + x22 + x33 ,

where all of them are corrupted by noise N (0, 0.01) to generate the target Y .

Models. To model the data-generating processes Flin , Fint , and Fadd , we randomly draw 10,000 samples and
fit correctly specified linear models with sklearn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), ensuring correctly learned effects for
each functional form. Input features are transformed to match the underlying functional relationships, with the
relevant main effects and interaction terms selected accordingly. This method guarantees that the models retain
linearity in the parameter space, consistent with the specified functional relationships. As a result, we obtain
an almost perfect fit across all three synthetic datasets, achieving R2 values greater than 0.99 on a separately
sampled test dataset of size 10,000 following the same distribution as defined above.

F.2    Models and Datasets for Real-World Examples

In addition to the synthetic datasets, we evaluate the performance of our models on four real-world datasets:
California housing, Bike sharing, titanic, and IMDB, including regression and classification tasks. In the fol-
lowing, we describe the datasets and the corresponding models. With the exception of IMDB, we fit models on
each dataset and evaluate the performance on regression tasks with the mean squared error (MSE) and the the
coefficient of determination (R2 ). For classification, we evaluate models using accuracy (ACC) and the F1 score.
All results are averaged over five Monte Carlo cross-validation runs to ensure robustness. We use XGBoost for
training gradient-boosted decision trees (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) and PyTorch for training neural networks
(Paszke et al., 2019). All other trained models and preprocessing steps are implemented with sklearn (Pe-
dregosa et al., 2011). In the case of the IMDB language data we do not fit a model but retrieve a pre-trained

F.3   Experimental Setup

We compute explanations for the models and datasets described in Appendices F.1 and F.2 based on the shapiq
(Muschalik et al., 2024a) python library. To do so, we create three kinds of cooperative explanation games, a
local explanation game (see Definition 4), a global risk game (see Definition 6), and a global sensitivity game (see
Definition 5). Both the global risk and global sensitivity games are based on a collection of independent local
explanation games. The local explanation game consists of a model, a data point, and an imputation strategy.
The imputation strategies follow the definitions of FS in the b-fANOVA, m-fANOVA, and c-fANOVA. To model
b-fANOVA, we compute mean feature values from a background dataset for each feature independently and
impute missing features with these baseline values. For m-fANOVA, we sample replacement data points directly
from a background dataset according to the joint marginal distribution. For the synthetic datasets and known
functional dependencies (see Appendix F.1), we compute the c-fANOVA with the ground-truth conditional
distributions. For real-world datasets and c-fANOVA, we rely on the conditional imputer implemented in the
shapiq package (Muschalik et al., 2024b). For marginal and conditional imputation we average over 512 sampled
replacement data points for each game evaluation. All imputation strategies operate on the test datasets.

G.1    Dissecting the Linear Models on Synthetic Data

Here, we provide more detailed explanations for the results of the synthetic experiments summarized in Section
5.1 as well as of the other two functional relationships defined in Appendix F.1. We start with explaining the
individual feature effects for Flin which is the basis for Fint being the model we analyzed in Section 5.1. We then
show how the additionally multiplicative interaction term in Fint influences each feature’s effect. Finally, we
demonstrate how individual effects for an additive (non-linear) main effect model Fadd differ from linear effects.
All explanations are compared based on the instance x = (1, 1, 1, 1).

Linear Main Effect Model. The left plot in Figure 5 illustrates the influence of feature distribution and of
higher-order interactions for Flin . It shows that the pure, partial, and full effects have the same value (main
effect) for all features for b- and m-fANOVA. This indicates that there are no feature interactions present
between any of the features. Since both decompositions break the dependencies between the feature of interest
and the remaining features in the dataset and due to a purely linear additive relationship of the features, feature
dependencies do not influence the individual effects. Feature x4 does not have an influence on the model and thus
has an individual effect of zero when b- or m-fANOVA are used. This changes for c-fANOVA since it accounts
for cross-correlation effects with other influential features. Therefore, the pure individual effects of c-fANOVA
are the same as the ones of b- and m-fANOVA for the uncorrelated setting, but adds to each individual effect the
effect of the remaining features by the factor ρ in the correlated settings. Hence, the interpretation of the pure
effect with c-fANOVA quantifies how much of the overall feature effects we account for if we add the feature of
interest to an empty model while considering the underlying feature distribution. It follows that the full effect
for ρ = 0.9 is almost zero for all features since the other three features have already been added to the model
and account for the effects of the remaining feature through cross-correlation effects.

Linear Model with Main and Interaction Effects. The right plot in Figure 5 is a reprint of the plot shown
in Section 5.1 for the model Fint . It shows that pure and partial m-fANOVA effects of x3 are affected by ρ. This
effect is caused by the interaction effect between x1 and x2 which influences the joint marginal distribution of
x−3 by their covariance and hence influences the pure and partial individual effects of the feature x3 . The partial
and full plots of b- and m-fANOVA clearly show that interactions are present between the first three features
with a higher interaction between x1 and x2 . C-fANOVA again accounts for cross-correlation effects and thus
shows the same pattern for pure effects as Flin , while indicating learned feature interactions for partial and full
effects.

Additive Main Effect Model. Figure 6 shows the local effects for Fadd . Comparable to Flin , b-fANOVA
is neither influenced by the feature distribution (ρ) nor by the higher-order interactions. While m-fANOVA is
equivalent to b-fANOVA for Flin , it differs for the still additive relationship, when effects are non-linear: The
effect of x2 for the instance x = (1, 1, 1, 1) becomes 0 since f1 (x) = E[Fadd (x2 , X−2 )] − E[F (X)] = x2 + E[X1 +
X33 ] − E[X1 + X22 + X33 ] = 0. However, as for b-fANOVA, pure, partial, and full effects are the same and thus
indicating that no feature interactions have been learned by the model. Due to accounting for cross-correlation
effects, x4 shows again an influence for pure and partial effects, when c-fANOVA is used. Moreover, full effects
with c-fANOVA show a similar vanishing trend as for Flin and Fint .
