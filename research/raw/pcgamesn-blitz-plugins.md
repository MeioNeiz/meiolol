# Despite new wave of third-party bans, League of Legends will still allow them

After Marvel Rivals banned Blitz, Riot has responded to concerns over its use in League of Legends, claiming there are "major differences."

[Lauren Bergin](https://www.pcgamesn.com/author/lauren-bergin) News Editor

Lauren spends most of her time dead in League of Legends, or equally as dead in Valorant. Don't ask her about Vampire: The Masquerade.

Published: February 26, 2025

On Friday February 21, NetEase announced that the use of third-party plugins - including Blitz.gg - would be viewed as cheating, effectively banning them entirely. This quickly raised questions around **League of Legends**, where Blitz, Porofessor, and other overlay systems are completely legal. While Riot cracks down hard on modifications to its MOBA (custom skins primarily), product lead Chris 'Auberaun' Roberts has confirmed that using Blitz won't incur any penalties.

If you're unfamiliar with Blitz.gg (I've _absolutely never used it_), it basically tracks objective spawns, ally summoner spells, and can recommend build pathways (including abilities). When you're loading into the [MOBA](https://www.pcgamesn.com/which-best-moba), you can also add an overlay to the lobby that gives you an overview of everyone's general strengths and weaknesses - your top laner may die early to ganks, for example.

Theoretically, Blitz doesn't give you any information that [League of Legends](https://www.pcgamesn.com/league-of-legends) itself doesn't: realistically you can time down objectives if you want to, as well as ally summoner spells. As a result, Riot has largely left it alone: Blitz repurposes the data Riot itself provides, after all.

Having read through the Marvel Rivals overlay that's available on the website right now, it also seems to track _your team_ specifically instead of providing information on your enemies. It shows your squad's health and ultimates, which are pingable in-game, as well as adding a real-time scoreboard to save you from having to hold down tab.

While it doesn't appear to be particularly invasive ([some](https://old.reddit.com/r/leagueoflegends/comments/1iy0uoo/marvel_rivals_has_now_officially_categorized_3rd/meqkg06/) do claim it used to show enemy ultimate charges, but that doesn't appear to be the case with the current version), NetEase isn't playing games: using Blitz.gg and other third-party plugins will net you a ban.

As a result, players have raised concerns around its use in League of Legends, with one post stating that "I personally would love for them to just get taken out of the game completely already, but I also know that some people don't believe that these are that big of an issue."

The general response seems to be that, if the plugin provides information that's to the detriment of the _enemy_ team, it should be banned. It's a sentiment that Roberts agrees with, but he clarifies that there are "major differences" between what Blitz shows for League of Legends, and what was available to Marvel Rivals players.

"From what other folks are pointing it out it sounds like there's some pretty major differences in how Blitz specifically interacted with Rivals vs how it does with League," he writes. "We agree though that having a tool that tracks enemy ult cooldowns crosses the line in providing information that's not available in game.

"Generally we don't want apps pulling in gameplay information that provides a competitive advantage that isn't just extrapolating from information we already provide (e.g. converting a radial timer to a numeric one). If it feels like you start to 'need' it to be competitive, then we're likely to take some action there." Blitz is here to stay, then - at least, for the moment.

# Nodusfall feels like Monster Hunter meets Elden Ring, and it's a new look for HoYoverse

I went hands-on with Nodusfall, HoYoverse's big new action game, at Gamescom, and came away impressed by HoYo's take on Monster Hunter.

[Tom Hopkins](https://www.pcgamesn.com/author/tom-hopkins) Guides Editor

Tom is an expert in all things Fortnite or the latest RPG release, but is probably spending too much time on the latest EA FC game.

Published: September 8, 2026

We've come to expect certain things from HoYoverse over the years. Gacha unlock systems, a cute anime style, and frequent updates. **Nodusfall**, the publisher's latest game that kicked off Gamescom Opening Night Live, is a huge departure from all of that, and I went hands-on with it to see exactly how it plays.

Your initial impressions after watching the reveal trailer are probably correct. Nodusfall is a Monster Hunter-like set in a dark fantasy world. The similarities with the likes of Elden Ring don't stop there, though. It's only coming to PC at launch (for now at least), and it's a multiplayer, combat-focused experience without any character gacha mechanics.

During my 30 minutes of time with the game, I feel like I only scratched the surface, to the point that I'm still not entirely sure what the game's structure is. Four other journalists and I picked a character each and set out on three different jobs. Said jobs took us through snippets of fantasy worlds to meet a boss. We then worked together to take the boss down before heading back to the game's hub world. We didn't explore the world, nor the hub area, so I don't know how open they are, but I can already tell that combat is going to be the star of the show in Nodusfall.

Combat feels a lot like Monster Hunter. Fights take place in arenas or open areas, much like in Capcom's game, and you're tasked with chipping away at the boss's massive health bar over the course of five or ten minutes. The first fight my team faced was against a monster in a river, the second was against a dragon in a spectacular shallow pool with a mysterious low roof, while the third took place in a huge open plain.

You and your crew sprint about, attacking when you get a chance, using your abilities to do more damage whenever they reload. Nodusfall's tutorial introduces a bunch of abilities to each character, based on button combinations and combos, each of which has a different use-case. For example, the close-range, tank-like character I was playing wielded a massive sword, which charged up as you landed attacks. Depending on which of the sword's three stages you charged it up to, you could hold the attack button to unleash an increasingly powerful lightning attack. There's a risk/reward aspect to your character's abilities and attacks, which ensures you're not simply mashing light and heavy attacks in the hope the giant monster you're fighting will eventually fall. There's a level of thought required in fights that I have often found absent in similar games. I also didn't feel like I was swinging away and not connecting, or starting an attack only to find the enemy is gone by the time it lands. That removes a level of frustration that other similar games can suffer from.

While HoYoverse insists that you _can_ play Nodusfall solo, it definitely feels like playing with three other friends is the way to go. Ranged characters are helpful, healers are vital, and communicating when to use each skill, depending on the monster's movement, is the key to success. HoYoverse says it's followed a "wide funnel philosophy" which creates a "low barrier to entry for new players, and a high skill ceiling to satisfy hardcore players." It also plans to extend out the already large suite of characters that'll come to the game, which will be designed to provide an option for everyone. The likes of Monster Hunter can feel overwhelming with all the systems and finicky mechanics, but I think Nodusfall will make the structure feel a little more welcoming.

When fighting the monsters, Nodusfall's similarities with Elden Ring shine bright. While HoYoverse says the game is "set in a world where different mythologies converge," it does look like standard dark fantasy fare at first glance. Giant creatures and huge knights with devastating swords are familiar enemies, and the way they move is extremely reminiscent of FromSoftware's open-world masterpiece. The huge sweeping attacks, the quick leaps around arenas, and multi-stage advances make bouts feel like facing Elden Ring's famous bosses, albeit on a larger stage without the risk of immediate death. Monster Hunter meets Elden Ring is a sell that has the potential to attract millions of people, but it'll be interesting to see how Nodusfall develops its own style to ensure it stands out.

As you'd expect from a HoYoverse game, though, Nodusfall is beautiful. The open landscapes are impressively detailed, but the boss stages are vast, unique, and majestic. Lightning storms rained down during one fight, adding to the atmosphere and muddying the visuals further. There's a scale to everything that'll be familiar to anyone who has played Genshin, but the darker tone makes sure it stands out.

I felt like I only scratched the surface of what Nodusfall will be at launch. The darker but more welcoming Monster Hunter structure will interest a lot of players, but the combat has a level of depth that keeps encounters exciting. It's a wild change for HoYoverse, but I'm intrigued to see how they manage a big, adult, multiplayer PC game for the first time.

# The Witcher 3 Songs of the Past isn't nostalgia bait - it's a welcome evolution of CDPR's iconic RPG

[Lauren Bergin](https://www.pcgamesn.com/author/lauren-bergin) News Editor

Lauren spends most of her time dead in League of Legends, or equally as dead in Valorant. Don't ask her about Vampire: The Masquerade.

Said Witcher, Geralt, awakens from an alcohol-induced, nightmare-ridden slumber, blinking as he looks up at a sunny sky, laughter and merriment drifting on the breeze. **Songs of the Past**'s live Gamescom demo looks a little different from what I expected, but it immediately has me intrigued.

[The Witcher 3](https://www.pcgamesn.com/the-witcher-3-wild-hunt)'s new DLC [focuses on Dandelion](https://www.pcgamesn.com/the-witcher-3-wild-hunt/songs-of-the-past-story-dandelion), or Jaskier, if you want his original Polish name. Geralt's faithful bard has gone missing, with his trusty lute turning up in the hands of mischief-makers. Our preview's guide chooses to give the drunks a good ol' smack, prompting them to quickly return said lute, but they don't seem to know where Dandelion is. After that beating, I reckon they'd tell us if they did.

Geralt sets off to Manor de Lettenhove to question Dandelion's family and try and work out where he's run off to. We're introduced to his fearsome grandmother, and his cousin, Lilla, a young woman hellbent on unshackling herself from her regal chains and heading out into the wilderness. After a brief conversation, it turns out that, in his alcohol-tinted dreams, Geralt has seen visions of an eerie well just outside of Letten, which is apparently haunted by an ancient witch. Lilla firmly believes that Dandelion has wound up there, but his grandmother remains stalwart in her view that he'll 'turn up eventually.' Our path forward is pretty clear: head to the well, and find out what's going on.

Songs of the Past's most obvious addition is, of course, Letten. The region is absolutely beautiful, with its emerald pastures juxtaposing perfectly against the dark, rotten feel of the witch's domain. "When we're building a new place on the map, the most important thing is to stick to the principles we already know," Creative Vision Director, Karolina Kuzia-Rokosz tells me. "The new place has to feel like it belongs, but is still fresh and different. We want to give you this experience of visiting a new place you haven't visited before; the idea is for you to feel at home, but also feel like this place is new."

Letten isn't all sunshine and rainbows, however; the witch's well is oppressive and claustrophobic, with thorny vines draining the lifeblood from wandering animals and unfortunate travelers. "We take extra care to make sure that these places are intertwined with each other and that there's a story behind them," Kuzia-Rokosz continues. "It's woven into what you can learn from the people of Letten: you hear about the fact that there is this place that they don't allow their children to play in because it holds a dark secret. But people don't understand it; it's not the main problem on their list. They live different lives and just don't mention it because, why would you?"

The 'out of sight, out of mind' mentality is immediately reminiscent, once again, of The Shire in The Lord of the Rings. It adds that sense of innocence, and in turn that sense that you _want_ to save Letten. The world of The Witcher is overridden with monsters and violence; darkness and shadow. Letten is a safe haven - a bastion of goodness, untouched by war. The parallels are clear, and after just an hour of live gameplay, I do find myself wanting to be Letten's Frodo.

Geralt and Lilla, however, simply can't resist the well's call, and head on out into the murky thickets in search of Dandelion. This is where we get our first taste of combat, with some fresh enemies (called Swarmions) to fight using Geralt's new chain weapon. As a result of the free remaster, fights feel smoother and more fluid, and the chain adds a new, dynamic element to how Geralt approaches combat. You can target individual parts of your foes, but in this instance we go straight for the throat, decapitating the corpse-like, corrupted beastie in an instant. It's smooth, it's clean, and most of all, it's sick as hell.

The chain is both a welcome throwback to The Witcher's extensive past and also a completely different look for Geralt. Your swords have always been your best friend, and while there are some ranged options, they quickly fall by the wayside as you level up. As someone who naturally chooses more ranged classes in videogames, the chain is a welcome addition, and has a certain level of gory finesse that the game's other weapons simply don't have.

The other new addition to our story is, of course, Lilla. Dandelion's cousin is feisty and fearless, but with that comes a level of naivety. Geralt's first instinct is to show no mercy, contrasting starkly with Lilla's humanity and innocence. To avoid spoiling things too much, during the final boss sequence the wooden, magic-fueled corpse appears to recognize her, whether directly or as a result of the royal blood that runs through her veins. There's a moment of tenderness as Lilla leans closer, attempting to communicate, but then the fight ensues.

Post-combat, there's the opportunity for a seemingly romantic moment between our Witcher and his new companion; you can pull her into a hug and comfort her - she's been through a hell of a lot in the past hour. "We really like how this character unfolds," Kuzia-Rokosz tells me. "She is really very exciting, and you can already feel from the demo that there's some sort of _tension_ between them \[Lilla and Geralt\], but I will leave the rest for the players to unfold. However, Lilla is a nice girl!" She starts to giggle.

This prompts a quick response from CD Projekt Red's PR Manager, who comments that people always ask if Lilla is romanceable, but not Dandelion's grandmother. In the interest of equality, I put that question to Kuzia-Rokosz, who laughs and replies, "Well, she's kind of the same age as Geralt, but oh, grandma would be a formidable foe, you don't mess with her! She has to be the head of the family; she is very strict, she is kind of serious." No romancing granny then. Unless?

What's perhaps more exciting than the demo itself is Kuzia-Rokosz and CD Projekt Red's passion. This is a team that _loves_ its IPs: they even had Witcher-inspired beers in their booth fridge that were, of course, brewed in Letten's local brewery (they were very good, by the way).

It's easy these days to return to a classic IP for easy, nostalgia-tinged cash. That isn't what's happening here: Songs of the Past is a new story, handcrafted with love; a welcome return to one of gaming's most beloved franchises. It's not a cheap, rosy throwback: it's a fond farewell to one of gaming's most iconic characters.

"Welcome back to the path!" Kuzia-Rokosz says with a huge smile, and man, I'm so happy to be here. Trust me when I say, you will be, too.

# Castlevania Belmont's Curse blends the classic Metroidvania's soul with Dead Cells' immaculate style

The new Castlevania game succeeds in being both familiar and fresh, and it’s easily the prettiest title I saw at Gamescom this year.

[Lauren Bergin](https://www.pcgamesn.com/author/lauren-bergin) News Editor

Lauren spends most of her time dead in League of Legends, or equally as dead in Valorant. Don't ask her about Vampire: The Masquerade.

Published: September 8, 2026

As I sit down to play **Castlevania: Belmont's Curse** at Gamescom, I'm immediately struck by just how colorful it is. Bright blood reds and eerie, noxious greens contrast sharply against dark, midnight blacks. We're in Paris, but not the one we know. This is a Paris fraught with monsters that lurk in the sewers and haunt the shadows. It doesn't feel like a place that should be colorful; I expected oppressive darkness and constant violence. Instead, I'm met with one of the most beautiful videogames I've ever seen - perhaps the most aesthetically pleasing of 2026. While Castlevania has always had style, Belmont's Curse takes that to a new level.

Carefully crafted by Dead Cells alumni Evil Empire, you're thrust into the shoes of Rose Belmont, the daughter of prolific vampire hunter, Trevor Belmont, and the seemingly now-deceased Sypha Belmont (or Belnades, if you're familiar with the Netflix show). Rose bears a striking resemblance to her mother - she's got red-tinted hair and sharp blue eyes - but it's clear that she fancies herself as more of Belmont-style brawler than a crafty mage. Dropped off in Paris by her father, she embarks on an adventure to hunt down all things evil, and it's immediately apparent that it won't be easy.

Now I'll admit that I struggle somewhat with [Metroidvanias](https://www.pcgamesn.com/best-metroidvania-games-pc). Hollow Knight pushed me to my limits, and even the side-scrolling action of Animal Well tested me more than I'd like to admit. [Belmont's Curse](https://www.pcgamesn.com/castlevania-belmonts-curse)'s first boss - The Fallen - handed me several defeats before I eventually felled it, but I'd argue that it never once felt frustrating. It took me a hot minute to learn its combinations - dodge whip, avoid or destroy boomerang with my spells, attack when he's tired himself out - but I found that, once you got used to him, it was easy enough to take him down. Sure, the extra health potions helped, but we're not keeping count here.

There's also a solid level of variation in the game's combat. While I didn't get to toy with a lot of the more advanced progression systems, being able to switch between standard melee combat and casting spells at range allowed me to consistently change up my approach to battle. As someone who naturally prefers to play at a distance versus up close and personal, the fluidity of being able to toss out a spell, then dive into the fray to soak up some mana perfectly fits with how I like to play Metroidvanias. It's fast-paced yet also familiar, and meant that my numerous attempts against the big angry Fallen didn't get repetitive and boring.

Even in more general, non-boss fight areas, combat continued to feel dynamic, with different enemies offering unique challenges. If I take the low level skeleton archers for example, running at them to close the distance then hacking them up does work, but they're all too happy to give you a swift boot to the gut to knock you back and volley you with arrows. It adds an extra dimension to the standard Metroidvania 'drive by and slash' combat, making foes feel both more reactive and actively harder to beat, even at lower levels. It establishes that Castlevania isn't for the faint of heart; it's going to test you, and you'd better get used to it.

If you're more interested in avoiding enemies, though, that's made a lot easier when you acquire the Belmont family's aptly named whip, Vampire Killer. While Castlevania and, to quote Belmont's Curse's Steam page, "satisfying whip action" go hand-in-hand, the whip has primarily been a weapon rather than a mobility device. We've seen it used as a grapple in the likes of Lords of Shadow (think Gabriel's Combat Cross), and there are similarities with Super Castlevania 4's Brandish Whip technique, but Evil Empire takes this to a whole new level.

Your whip is your primary traversal device and, by proxy, your new best friend. You can use it to sweep over the heads of foes to streamline your path to the next boss battle (Metroidvanias are terrible for making you constantly run back and forth), or you can fire yourself into the air to meet your foes in the sky. Flying enemies no longer stand a chance, and Evil Empire does a great job of integrating the whip's movement into Paris' various traps and puzzles.

The whip adds both a new element to combat, making things feel faster and more dynamic, while also addressing some of the primary traversal woes that Metroidvanias face. Spawning miles away from a boss doesn't feel _as_ bad when you can bypass all of the enemies to get there. But, you can also take the time to send them back to hell if you really want to, with a little bit of added style.

I have to mention the game's soundtrack. Some music just sends shivers down your spine; it makes you feel _something_, even if you don't quite know what that something is. That's exactly what I felt with Belmont's Curse; as soon as I put on the headphones (which were, admittedly, top of the range), the eerie, Dracula-esque harpsichord consumed me. I genuinely felt like I was a vampire hunter, swinging through the streets of Paris, destroying anything that dared to get in my way. There was a sense of real power - it's not anthemic, but it has the same confidence-boosting effect.

Coupled with the environments, the soundtrack helps portray a genuine sense of scale - everything feels big and momentous. It's a masterclass in art and sound design, and if Konami doesn't release a vinyl version when the game launches, I'll probably riot - and we all know how French revolutions end.

Belmont's Curse does a lot right, but the thing that's most exciting is that it feels like it brings Castlevania into the modern day. The game has Evil Empire's DNA; you can see Dead Cells' influence in its visuals and difficulty. Yet, it's quintessentially Castlevania. Straddling that balance and making everything feel cohesive and familiar isn't easy, but Evil Empire has done it in style.

At Gamescom, Evil Empire's Marketing Director, Bérenger Dupré, and Lead Level Designer, Sandro Bordier, [told me](https://www.pcgamesn.com/castlevania-belmonts-curse/interview-gamescom-2026) that they were "honored by how much trust" Konami has put in their vision. Konami's Tsutomu Taniguchi echoed that the publisher has "huge trust" in the Evil Empire team. I can see where that's come from. What EE has done is modernize a classic, yet keep its spirit.

For the first time in years, I'm hyped for a Castlevania game. I'm excited to see how the team has imagined the likes of Carmilla in its own unique style, and to play more further down the line.
