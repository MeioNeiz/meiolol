Abstract
Estimating win probabilities in multiplayer online battle arena games like Dota 2 provides actionable insights for players on in-game events. However, it remains challenging due to high-dimensional and rapidly evolving game states. This thesis presents a
two-stage, time-slice-based machine learning pipeline for estimating teams’ win probabilities throughout the game. First, a Transformer-based encoder consumes sequences
of fixed-interval snapshots of the game states. Then, it is trained in a self-supervised
manner on the next token prediction task to internalize game dynamics. Second, a
lightweight MLP classifier maps the final embedding given by the encoder to a winning probability of one of the teams. The combined model achieves high accuracy and
well-calibrated probability estimates, giving responsive win-probability outputs that
adapt to pivotal in-game events such as team fights, objective captures, and dramatic
comebacks.

Introduction
1.1

Motivation

During the 21st century, computer games have become one of the top ways for people
to spend their free time. They have evolved from simple pastimes into a global cultural
phenomenon, captivating millions of players across all ages and demographics.
Beyond casual entertainment, many individuals pursue gaming as a competitive
endeavor, encouraging them to refine their gameplay by improving their reflexes, teamplay, and critical thinking abilities. This competitive spirit has given rise to e-sports
and professional gaming tournaments that gather full stadiums of people, attract substantial sponsorship, and have significant media coverage. As competition grows, both
professional and amateur players seek ways to surpass skill thresholds, forcing them to
adapt and utilize data-driven training methods. Simply playing the game is no longer
sufficient, making targeted practice and analysis of performance patterns crucial in
achieving meaningful improvements. To improve strategic decision-making, a player
should understand exactly why a match has ended in a win or a loss.
This thesis proposes a way of using in-game metrics to analyze matches in Dota 2
1 and estimating winning probabilities throughout the game, highlighting key events
that happened during the match, offering players a way to extract actionable insights
to accelerate their skill development and competitive success.

1.2

Domain Overview

1.2.1

What is Dota 2

Dota 2 is a computer game of the MOBA genre, where two teams, Radiant and Dire,
consisting of 5 players, face each other with the goal of defeating the opposing team.
Each match takes place on a square map divided into three lanes, with the objective
of destroying the enemy’s Ancient, both of which are placed in the opposite corners of
the map, while protecting their own. The game requires strategic thinking, teamwork,
and individual skills, as players select unique heroes with special abilities to outsmart
and overpower their opponents, leading to a victory.

interpret the game. In-client tools provide basic graphs for net worth (accumulated

Problem Definition

Win probability prediction in Dota 2 is the task of estimating the likelihood that a
given team will win the match based on the current state of the game. Formally,
at any discrete time t in a match, given the sequence of game states up to t, the
goal is to compute P (win|seqt ) for one of the teams (e.g., Radiant). This problem is
challenging because Dota 2’s game state is high-dimensional, and the game’s dynamics
are complex and nonlinear. Sometimes, small advantages can snowball into significant
leads, and one team fight can turn the tables and drastically reverse the lead.
Existing systems like the Dota Plus win probability graph use undisclosed proprietary models to make these predictions. The way to address this problem is to use
time-slice sequences of game data as input and predict the outcome probability as
output. A core challenge is capturing the temporal aspect of how the game evolves
over time. Instead of treating each state in isolation, the model must understand
sequential patterns. Therefore, the problem can be seen as a sequence modeling task
with a prediction target at the end of the sequence representing a team’s probability
of winning the game.

1.4

Aim and Objectives

The primary aim of this work is to develop a machine learning-based system that
predicts win probabilities throughout a Dota 2 match and to do so in a way that is
accurate, interpretable, and accessible. To achieve this aim, the following objectives
are set:
• Data Collection and Processing
Gather a substantial dataset of Dota 2 replays, parse them into time-slice data
containing information about the state of the game at the time of the snapshot,
and structure it in a format suitable for machine learning.
• Feature Engineering
Using parsed data provided by the game engine, turn raw statistics into features
with more descriptive power, such as turning positions of heroes on the map into
features that describe the whole team coordination.
• Developing a Modeling Approach
Develop a sequence modeling approach for win probability estimation by utilizing a neural network to encode sequences of game states that will capture the
temporal dynamics of Dota 2 matches. Then, using encoded sequences, train a
supervised classifier that outputs the probability of the Radiant team winning.
• Benchmarking and Evaluation
Evaluate the model’s performance against existing tools.
By successfully meeting the outlined objectives, this project will create a proofof-concept analytics tool for Dota 2. The goal is not only to accurately predict match
outcomes but also to improve players’ learning experiences by offering an accessible
method for detailed game analysis.

Most research on Dota 2 outcome prediction follows two main lines. Early draft–only
studies encode each hero as a one-hot vector and apply simple classifiers such as Naı̈ve
Bayes, yielding roughly 60–70% accuracy [9].
Later work expands those vectors with hand-engineered hero attributes and treebased models, pushing pregame accuracy past 70% [10]. Another works use live game
state data: recurrent models that ingest minute-by-minute game state achieve 90%
accuracy within the first few minutes of play [1].
The newer approach treats the hero draft order as a sentence, learning wordto-vector hero embeddings and using attention-based LSTMs for modest gains over
earlier draft-only baselines [8]. Overall, accuracy improves as representations move
from static hero rosters to richer descriptors and real-time game dynamics.
Some authors also developed a framework for time-slice evaluation of MOBA games
and applied it to Dota 2, comparing it to Dota Plus, assistant, and claimed to achieve
a slightly higher prediction accuracy [11]. However, their modeling approach does
not capture the game’s temporal dependencies, as they treat game snapshots independently and use a discounted evaluation function, which depends both on who will
win and how much time remains until the game ends instead of the ground truth for
training their model.
OpenAI [5] trained a self-play agent that achieved superhuman performance, whose
LSTM backbone was augmented with a win probability estimation head, producing
well-calibrated victory estimates during matches. They also defined a rich observation
space consisting of global timers, per-hero stats, per-unit positions, item cooldowns,
and more. Their success reveals two lessons: sequence models can track long-term
credit assignments, and a broad, structured feature set supports accurate win forecasts.

1.5

Player Skill Analysis

Drachen et al. [2] provided a large-scale study of how team cohesion and frequency of
changing zones correlate with players’ skills in Dota 2. By clustering hero positions,
they showed that professional teams maintain tighter formations and switch map
zones more often than their lower-tier peers. These spatial patterns often prepare the
ground for decisive team fights or objective trades, suggesting that explicit spatial
metrics, such as average pairwise hero distance, could provide valuable information to
models estimating win probability.
Pengmatchaya and Natwichai [6] proposed a pipeline that uses OpenDota to extract three types of features: basic stats (kills, deaths, assists), tactical decisions
(team-fight participation, lane efficiency), and spatio-temporal coverage (map zones
change, team spread) to predict a player’s skill tier. Their results show that richly
engineered behavioral and spatial descriptors substantially boost the quality of predictions. Although their modeling target is estimating player skill level rather than game
outcome, many of their features are directly relevant to estimating on-field advantage
since higher-skilled teams transform map control into game lead more reliably.
These studies collectively show that rich and time-aware representations of game
states and player behaviors drive the strongest predictions in Dota 2 analytics. Beyond
outcome prediction, explicit modeling of team cohesion and spatial spread demonstrate that both map control and small tactical decisions are crucial. Finally, the
power of sequence models and broad observation spaces are highlighted as beneficial
for good win estimates.

Theoretical Background
2.1

Game Mechanics

The analysis conducted in this work is based on version 7.37 of Dota 2. At this point
in the game’s evolution, players can choose from 124 unique heroes and purchase over
200 items, creating many strategic possibilities.
In addition to the primary objective of destroying the enemy Ancient, players
must account for a variety of secondary objectives across the map, including towers,
barracks, active runes, and the powerful neutral boss Roshan, killing which gives a
significant advantage by providing a second life for a single player as well as gold and
experience for the whole team.
Each player is commonly assigned to a role such as carry, support, offlaner, and
midlaner, and success depends not only on individual skill but also on effective team
composition and coordination. Throughout the game, players earn experience to level
up their heroes and accumulate gold to purchase items that make them stronger.
The Dota 2 map features a dynamic day and night cycle, which alters map visibility
throughout the game. Players must utilize observer and sentry wards, objects that
allow vision control across the environment, which is critical for map dominance by
providing additional information.
During the match’s early stages, players participate in the laning phase, where they
position themselves along one of the three lanes: top, middle, or bottom. Each lane is
defended by three towers sequentially placed along the lane and waves of computercontrolled units, known as creeps, that spawn every 30 seconds at the beginning of the
lane. Players aim to secure "last hits" on enemy creeps to gain gold while denying the
same to opponents, all while avoiding taking excessive damage from enemy heroes.
The laning phase is critical for setting the game’s pace, allowing players to accumulate
resources, and establishing early advantages, allowing a better map control.
In addition to lane creeps, the map contains camps of neutral creeps located in
the jungle areas between lanes. These neutral units are not aligned with either team
and provide opportunities for heroes to farm gold and experience outside the lanes.
Contesting and controlling access to jungle areas is thus a significant part of the
mid-game and late-game stages.
Moreover, resource management is amplified through active runes that spawn at
fixed intervals at specific locations on the river, dividing the map into two halves.
These runes provide powerful temporary effects, such as regeneration, double damage,
or invisibility, and securing them can swing fights in a team’s favor.
Effectively managing laning efficiency, jungle control, vision placement, and securing objectives shapes the gameplay of Dota 2. Teams that can coordinate their
movements, optimize resource collection, and take advantage of their opponents’ errors are often rewarded with success. The intricacy of Dota 2 arises from its vast
selection of heroes, diverse range of items, and the ongoing necessity for real-time

2.2

Transformer models

Transformer models, introduced in the landmark paper "Attention is All You Need"
[7], are the dominant architecture for sequence modeling tasks across various fields,
including natural language processing, computer vision, and time-series prediction.
At the core of the Transformer lies the attention mechanism, which allows the
model to weigh the importance of different elements within a sequence when making predictions. In particular, the self-attention operation computes a weighted sum
of all input elements for each position in the sequence, enabling the model to focus
on relevant input parts dynamically regardless of their distance. This approach contrasts with recurrent neural networks, which process sequences sequentially and often
struggle with long-range dependencies due to vanishing gradients.
Each Transformer layer typically consists of two main components: a multi-head
self-attention module and a position-wise feed-forward network. The multi-head mechanism allows the model to attend to information from different representation subspaces simultaneously, enhancing its ability to model complex relationships.
For sequence modeling, the attention mechanism provides a key advantage of capturing both local and global relationships, making Transformers particularly effective
for tasks where the entire sequence context is important for accurate prediction.
In the context of modeling time-evolving game states, such as in Dota 2, the Transformer architecture is well-suited due to its capacity to model complex, non-linear
interactions between multiple entities across consecutive time steps. By encoding the
state of the game at each moment and allowing attention across past observations,
the model can infer patterns and predict future outcomes with high fidelity.

2.3

Multi-Layer Perceptron Classifier

A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is a feed-forward neural network for classification
and regression tasks. It consists of multiple layers of neurons: an input layer that
takes features, one or more hidden layers that perform nonlinear transformations, and
an output layer that produces the final prediction. Each neuron in a layer is fully
connected to the neurons in the next layer, and an activation function like ReLU is
applied to introduce non-linearity. In theory, an MLP with even a single hidden layer
can model any continuous relationship between inputs and output given sufficient
neurons.
In the context of this thesis, the MLP plays the role of the win probability predictor
once given an encoded representation of the game state. After the transformer has
processed a sequence of time slices and produced a feature vector for the current state,
this vector is fed into the MLP. The MLP then outputs a single value between 0 and
1, representing the probability that the Radiant team will win the match. A value
near 1 means Radiant will almost certainly win, and near 0 means that Dire is likely
to win.

2.4

Sequence Modeling and Next Token Prediction

Predicting win probability in a time-sliced approach can be viewed through the lens
of sequence modeling. In sequence modeling, whether applied to time series or human

Chapter 2. Theoretical Background

7

language, there is one common training strategy for next token prediction. Here, the
model is trained to use an observed sequence of game states to predict the next state.
Each time-slice’s game state is treated as a token in this setting. The Transformer
is trained to predict the state at time t given the states at times t−c, t−c−1, . . . , t−1,
where c is the size of the context window.
Once pretraining via next-token prediction is complete, only the Transformer’s
encoder is retained to encode the input sequence into embeddings. These embeddings
are assumed to encapsulate the critical factors of game dynamics that determine
future state evolution and features that should also be highly informative for win/loss
probability estimation.
This approach mirrors the training of large language models, which learn representations through next-word prediction and are subsequently fine-tuned or used for
classification by leveraging those learned embeddings. In the proposed pipeline, the
pre-trained Transformer embeddings feed into an MLP classifier (see Section 2.3),
which is then trained on the actual match outcome.
Next-token training provides a form of self-supervised—pretraining on replay data.
By mastering short-term forecasting, the model gains a rich internal understanding of
temporal patterns in Dota 2, enabling the supervised probability estimation task to
be more data efficient and accurate.

Chapter 3

Data
3.1

Collecting Match Data

The primary data source for this work is the OpenDota API1 because of its extensive documentation and open-source nature. OpenDota allows API users to send
SQL queries addressed directly to their database containing public data on recorded
matches of Dota 2, making it easy to access necessary records.
The collected dataset contains 12000 pro league matches played on the 7.37 version
of the game. Each sample contains the following fields:
• match_id: A unique identifier is assigned to each match. It is used to distinguish individual games.
• match_seq_num: The sequence number of the match relative to all matches
played globally reflects the chronological order in which games were recorded.
• replay_salt: A number associated with the match that, together with the
match ID, can be used to retrieve the correct replay file.
• patch: The version of the game at the time the match was played. Game
patches introduce balance changes, new features, and bug fixes, and can significantly affect gameplay dynamics.
• game_mode: A numerical code indicating the type of match played.
• duration: The total length of the match in seconds, measuring how long the
game lasted from start to finish.
• start_time: The Unix timestamp marking when the match began.
• cluster: The server cluster ID where the match was hosted corresponds to a
specific geographical region.
• radiant_win: A boolean value indicating the outcome of the match, where
true means the Radiant team won and false means the Dire team won.

3.2

Processing Replay Files

The replay files need to be downloaded and parsed to extract in-game match data.
Having match ID, replay salt, and server cluster ID, a URL of where the compressed
replay file is stored can be constructed. After downloading the file, it must be decompressed and parsed using a specialized replay parser that can interpret Dota 2’s
engine internal messages.

The replay contains a chronological sequence of game state updates, player actions,

3.3

Developing a Replay Parser

There are some existing Dota 2 replay parser implementations, for example, OpenDota’s parser2 , but none of them had the required functionality of extracting structured sequential game state snapshots. Therefore, it was necessary to implement a
custom solution. For this task, a dedicated parser was developed using the open-source
clarity parsing framework3 . The resulting implementation had capabilities of reading every in-game tick4 and aggregating the gathered data into structured sequential
snapshots of the game state.
Each parsed replay is serialized as a single JSON object whose top-level keys
correspond to in-game time in seconds. Consecutive keys differ by 30 since it was
a chosen frequency for sampling the state of the game. For every timestamp t, the
value is a dictionary with two symmetric entries, radiant and dire, each encoding
the complete state of the respective team:
• teamId: internal identifier (2 for Radiant, 3 for Dire).
• players: an array of five player objects. Each player object stores
– static identifiers (key, steamId, name, heroId, heroVariant);
– economy-related features (networth, totalGold, currentGold);
– progression features (level, xp, kills, deaths, assists, lastHits, denies);
– combat metrics (heroDamage, towerDamage, damageTakenPreReduction,
damageTakenPostReduction, healing, teamfightParticipation);
– status flags (lifeState, respawnSeconds, buybackCooldown);
– ward placing statistics (obsPlaced, senPlaced);
– spatial coordinates (x, y) in Dota 2’s internal map system;
– inventory: a list of up to six item slots, each storing id, slot index, and
(where applicable) charge counts.
• buildings: a dictionary containing the states of every destroyable structure
belonging to the corresponding team: 11 towers, 3 pairs of barracks, and the
Ancient. Each entry records the remaining health of the structure.
• observerWards: a list of ward locations, each given by (x,y) coordinates.
• totalCampsStacked, totalRunePickups, totalTowersKilled, totalRoshansKilled, totalSmokesUsed: cumulative team-level counters up to the given
time-slice t.
This schema yields a time-ordered sequence of raw game state snapshots, enabling
downstream models to access local per-hero and global team and objectives features.

3.4

Parsed Replays Postprocessing

After parsing the replay files, they still contain some redundant information that
does not contain descriptive power for estimating winning probabilities, like players’
account names or names of players’ hero units that carry essentially the same information as the hero IDs. These keys are removed from the game state representations, and
only essential informative fields suitable for modeling remain. Removed keys include:
• From each player object:
– key, value, team: internal slot indices and team flags
– name, steamId: account identifiers
– heroVariant, unit: redundant hero-model strings
– damageTakenPreReduction: only post-reduction damage is kept
– slot, num_charges: inventory items metadata, keeping only the item’s
unique name identifier
• From the buildings dictionary:

4.2.7

Design Rationale

Self-attention captures long-range dependencies more effectively than recurrent models, and the concatenation strategy preserves explicit per-hero and per-team granularity while allowing the Transformer to learn cross–entity dependencies through
self-attention. A relatively deep encoder plus moderate hidden size balances capacity
and training speed. GELU activations match current best practices and improve convergence over ReLU. Self-supervised learning on abundant unlabeled replays reduces
reliance on labeled data and forces the model to learn general game dynamics rather
than trying to fit end-game statistics.

4.3

Win Probability Predictor

The outcome predictor is a lightweight MLP that maps the Transformer’s d-dimensional
embedding to a scalar logit. Its architecture is the following:
• Input: ht ∈ Rd : Transformer hidden size 512.

• Hidden layer: one Linear layer of 128 units, followed by GELU activation and
Dropout(p = 0.1).
• Output layer: one Linear unit with no activation, producing logit zt .
• Prediction: pt = σ(zt ) ∈ [0, 1] is the Radiant win probability (Dire win probability would calculate as 1 − pt ).

5.1

Training Models

5.1.1

Transformer

The Transformer is trained for a fixed number of 11 epochs using the Adam optimizer
with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 . At each epoch, the model processes all training
batches in a self-supervised manner, predicting the next slice after a given sequence
and minimizing the mean squared error of the numeric fields of the subsequent time
slice. The train-validation split is 85% to 15%. After each epoch, the average training
and validation losses are recorded (see Appendix A.2, Figure A.1).

5.1.2

MLP

Training is performed for 3 epochs using the Adam optimizer and the learning rate
10−3 using BCE loss with logits:


L = − y log σ(z) + (1 − y) log(1 − σ(z)) ,
where y ∈ {0, 1} is the final-outcome label.
At inference, for each time-slice embedding given a preceding window of size c − 1,
the trained MLP estimates win probability. For the outcome prediction given the last
20 slices of the game, the trained model achieves 99% accuracy on both training and
validation sets (see Appendix A.2, Figure A.2).

5.3

Inference Quality Assessment

5.3.1

Definition of a Good Probability Curve

Quantitative evaluation of win-probability curves against a "ground truth" is inherently problematic since there is no provider of the true winning probability at each
moment in a match. While final outcome labels allow the score of a binary classifier
at the game end, intermediate probabilities have no objective reference. In particular:
• No true time-series labels. There is only an observation of one team winning
or losing at tend , leading to an absence of measurements such as "Radiant had
71% chance of winning at minute 29."
• Subjectivity of estimation. Human experts may disagree on when a lead is
decisive. What feels like an "80% win chance" to one analyst may be "60%" to
another.

Chapter 5. Experiments and Results

18

Accordingly, in this section, an assessment of inference quality is performed via
case studies and visual inspection:
• Event responsiveness. Representative matches should demonstrate that the
curve reacts sharply to key events (team fights, Roshan kills, barracks destructions) and remains stable during low-action intervals.
• Monotonic trends. In one-sided games, the win probability should advance
steadily toward 1 or 0 without erratic reversals.
• Comeback detection. Matches with major lead swings should reveal that the
curve reflects both the timing and magnitude of comebacks, rising appropriately
after pivotal turning points.
• Early-game uncertainty. During the game’s first minutes, when statistical
advantages are minimal, the curve should remain close to 0.5, diverging only
after clear resource advantage or kill differentials emerge.
Ensuring that these qualitative aspects are present would suggest that the model’s
outputs are interpretable, actionable, and consistent with intuitive notions of match
dynamics, even without definitive time-series ground truth.

5.3.2

Case Studies

Case 1: Early-game uncertainty, Event responsiveness.
Key in-game events, such as team fights, Roshan kills, and objective destructions,
should provoke shifts in the win-probability curve. Consider match 81566714861 where
two pro teams face each other in a tournament, a relatively balanced game until a
big team-fight engagement started at minute 17. A minute before, the probability
hovers around the area of 0.5, followed by a team fight at the beginning of which
team Dire takes two kills. The curve leans to favor the Dire team. However, within
the next minute, team Radiant retaliates by chasing down and killing three enemy
heroes, reflecting the sudden swing back into Radiant’s advantage. At minute 25,
team Radiant destroys the enemy tier 2 middle tower, places observer wards granting
vision deep into the enemy’s territory, and shortly after gets two more kills. The
Radiant’s win probability then drastically shifts to a high number, reflecting earned
advantage. During the period from minute 27 to minute 28, the Dire team kills the
enemy midlaner, takes Roshan, and then also kills Radiant’s offlaner, which makes
the game even again. Numerous other skirmishes, objective contests, and resource
plays occurred throughout the game, each contributing to the curve’s fluctuations, for
example, the Dire team’s failed offense on Radiant’s base high ground at minute 46
(Figure 5.3).
This behavior demonstrates that the model not only tracks gradual resource accumulation but also reacts promptly to high-impact moments, aligning probability

Chapter 6

Conclusions
6.1

Achievements

• End–to–end pipeline. A complete pipeline was implemented that converts
raw Dota 2 replay files into time-aligned numerical snapshots, encodes sequential
context with a custom Transformer, and produces calibrated win-probability
curves through a lightweight MLP classifier.
• Replay parser and dataset. More than 11000 professional matches were
collected, parsed at 30-second resolution, and enriched with engineered spatial
features, yielding a time-slice dataset of considerable size.
• Self-supervised pre-training. The next-slice prediction was demonstrated as
a practical approach to training the encoder without relying on outcome labels
and providing expressive embeddings for downstream tasks.
• Qualitative fidelity. Case studies showed sharp event responsiveness, comeback detection, monotonic convergence in one-sided games, and early-game uncertainty behaviors aligned with expectations defined using domain knowledge.
They also compared the presented approach to the in-game paid feature Dota
Plus assistant.
• Probabilistic calibration. Temperature scaling of logits reduced the overconfidence of the model and produced well-calibrated probabilities.

6.2

Room for Improvement

• Extending early-game coverage. Since the first 20 slices are required to fill
the context window, no predictions are produced before minute 10. Introducing
a simple startup predictor that would generate probabilities based on heroes’
draft and landing statistics, such as harassing and resource managing, until the
whole Transformer’s context window is available, would solve this problem.
• Patch generalisation. The model is trained on a single patch. Investigating
continual learning techniques may improve robustness across game updates.
• Richer state encoding. Augment each slice with additional game signals, such
as hero ability cooldowns, buff/debuff statuses, and precise creep wave metrics
to capture a broader tactical context.

Chapter 6. Conclusions

6.3

Final Thoughts

This thesis demonstrates that modern sequence models and self-supervised learning
can extract actionable insight from complex, high-dimensional e-sports data. Although qualitative evaluation suggests parity with proprietary solutions, substantial
headroom remains—particularly in cross-patch robustness and objective probability
curve scoring. This research establishes a reproducible foundation upon which subsequent work can iterate, introducing roads for deeper strategic analysis, real-time
broadcasting tools, and cross-title generalization within multiplayer online battle arenas.
