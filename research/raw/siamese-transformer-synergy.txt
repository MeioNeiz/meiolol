Abstract
Predicting match outcomes typically depends on rich performance statistics. This work investigates whether it is possible
to extract team synergy from only team composition and use
it for sports outcome prediction. We model each player as
a token and use a Siamese transformer to learn within-team
interactions and compare team representations to predict the
win probability. Across multiple datasets, the model consistently outperforms classical baselines such as multilayer
perceptrons and non-Siamese transformers. Results suggest
that synergy between teammates can be captured from team
composition alone. When optional auxiliary information is
available, a parallel Siamese branch yields additional performance gains. Overall, this study shows that team composition
alone contains meaningful predictive structure that modern
attention-based models can effectively extract.

Sports outcome prediction is the task of predicting a game’s
outcome based on information such as team composition,
player statistics, skill assessments, past performance, and
environmental factors. Human experts often rely on news
reports, historical trends, and their intuition to make predictions (Jones et al., 2021). These predictions, while insightful,
can vary significantly in accuracy due to biases, limited data
access, or subjective judgments (Park et al., 2022).
The machine learning community commonly formulates
sports outcome prediction as a classification task. A wide
range of models—including Bayesian approaches (SantosFernandez, Wu, and Mengersen, 2019), random forests
(Smithies et al., 2021), and neural networks (Xenopoulos and Silva, 2021)—have been developed to incorporate
player and team statistics as well as contextual factors such
as home advantage and weather conditions (Bunker and Susnjak, 2022).
However, minimal research focuses only on team synergy.
Considering a basketball team composed of five excellent
point guards, even if each individual player has high performance ratings, the team would struggle in a real game because it would lack size for rebounding and rim protection,
suffer against bigger matchups, and miss the role diversity
needed for balanced, effective team play.

This work proposes the unique perspective that a transformer’s attention mechanism can capture team synergy directly from team composition: each player is treated as a token whose query, key, and value representations correspond
to the support the player seeks, provides, and contributes
within a team. To examine this hypothesis, a Siamese transformer architecture is constructed and evaluated across diverse datasets, including an NBA dataset, two League of
Legends datasets, a Valorant dataset, and two synthetic
benchmarks. The results indicate that attention mechanisms
can learn meaningful teammate interaction patterns, which
can contribute to accurate game outcome prediction.

Related Work
Transformer
The Transformer architecture is originally developed for
processing sequential data through self-attention mechanisms (Vaswani et al., 2017). In an encoder-only configuration, the encoder consists of stacked layers, each containing
a multi-head self-attention mechanism and a position-wise
feedforward network. The multi-head self-attention mechanism computes attention across all tokens in the sequence,
allowing the model to capture both local and global dependencies.
Existing studies have effectively utilized transformers for
predicting game outcomes. Nguyen and Santamaria (2020)
represents players as a vector of their historical numerical
statistics and uses transformers to learn the relationship between an individual player and their respective statistics to
forecast a team’s point production. Simpson et al. (2022)
uses a transformer to predict the next match event by learning from sequential game states and event histories, capturing complex temporal dependencies and contextual information with a transformer. Based on the recently developed
TCDformer: A transformer framework for non-stationary
time series forecasting (Wan et al., 2024), Liu et al. (2024)
proposed using TCDformer to learn a momentum encoding
module and use it to predict long-term sports outcomes.
The novelty of this study lies in its focus on team composition, treating each player as a token and leveraging the
transformer’s attention mechanism to uncover synergistic interactions within a team.

Sports Outcome Prediction using AI
Various AI models have been used to predict the outcome
of a sports game. Bayesian models (Santos-Fernandez, Wu,
and Mengersen, 2019), random forests (Smithies et al.,
2021), and neural networks (Xenopoulos and Silva, 2021)
are used to process player and team statistics, along with
environmental factors such as home advantage and weather
conditions (Bunker and Susnjak, 2022). Beal et al. (2021)
uses statistical match data and sports journal articles to predict the outcomes of the football matches.
(E)Sport datasets often contain detailed player, team, and
game statistics. Choosing an adequate subset of the given
features is challenging for a few reasons (Islam et al., 2022):
(1) The process of feature selection is susceptible to bias in
humans or the selection method used (Davis et al., 2024;
FiveThirtyEight, 2019). (2) Some features may inadvertently reveal details about the result of a game, causing data
leakage (Larsen, 2018). (3) Certain attributes are concealed
and are not adequately reflected by current features.
Given the prominent role of feature selection in sports
outcome prediction, a natural question arises: Is it possible
to construct a machine learning model that can identify team
synergy among players and predict game outcomes with sufficient accuracy while minimally relying on detailed player-,
team-, and game-level statistics?

Synthetic Dataset We generated two artificial datasets in
order to simulate a team-based game where team composition directly determines game outcome. Each character is
a feature vector of 3 randomly generated attributes h =<
k, q, v >: a key k ∈ [0, 1] represents the kind of support the
character provides to its teammates, a query q ∈ [0, 1] represents the kind of support it seeks from teammates, and a
value v ∈ [1, 4] represents a character’s overall skill level.
The character vector design is directly motivated by Query,
Key, Value (QKV) Attention (Vaswani et al., 2017).
Let a team T be composite of p characters, T =
{h1 , h2 , ..., hp }. A character hi ’s final performance pi is a
reflection of its skill after being supported by its teammates
in team T as:
pi = Σj̸=i (1 − |qi − kj |)vi vj
When qi and kj are close, hi is maximally supported by
hj by the amount up to vi vj ; when qi and kj are drastically
different, for example qi = 1 and kj = 0, hi is minimally
supported. Note that the support is not mutual, as the support
for hj from hi depends on |qj − ki | instead.
Lastly, a team’s performance T p is the summation of individual characters’ performance: T p = Σi pi .
Each game is a match-up between two teams, and the
team with higher performance wins the game. In summary,
a team’s performance is the sum of all team members’ performance. Each team member’s performance is amplified by
teammates who offer compatible support.
Using this design of the team-based game, we created two
datasets: (1) The Heroes10 dataset with 10 random characters, 1000 games of two teams, and each team is a random
set of 3 characters; (2) The Heroes20 dataset with 20 random characters, 1000 games of two teams, and each team is
a random set of 10 characters. Heroes20 can be considered
a more complicated version of Heroes10.
To avoid ambiguity in terminology, the members of a
team are referred to as “characters” throughout this work, regardless of whether they correspond to players (e.g., in basketball) or heroes (e.g., in LoL, Valorant, or Heroes10/20).

Data Preprocessing
All datasets follow the same format after collection. Each
sample concatenates the two teams’ composition tokens
(length 2p) and, when available, auxiliary features for both
teams. The label indicates whether team 1 wins.
Label embeddings for characters. A label encoder and
an embedding layer maps each character to a continuous vector representation. Similar to natural-language applications (Mikolov et al., 2013), these embedding layers
can learn through training to capture semantic relationships
among entities.
Down-sampling Before training, all datasets were downsampled to ensure an equal number of wins and losses, reducing class imbalance and mitigating potential bias.

Methods
Models for Comparison
The following architectures are evaluated for modeling team
compositions [ c1 , c2 ] and, when applicable, auxiliary information [ a1 , a2 ]. Among these, only Model 5-7 utilize auxiliary features, whereas others rely exclusively on team composition. Throughout, de denotes the embedding dimension,
p the number of characters per team, and V the size of
the character vocabulary. All models employ an embedding
layer that maps each character to an 8-dimensional embedding vector (de = 8). All transformer-based architectures
use a single TransformerEncoder layer with two attention
heads and a feed-forward dimension of 64.
1. Neural Network. Character embeddings for both teams
are concatenated into a vector of length 2pde . This vector
is processed by three fully connected layers with ReLU
activations, containing (2pde , 16, 8) units, respectively.
The final layer outputs a scalar corresponding to the predicted probability that team 1 wins.
2. Siamese Network. Each team composition ct is embedded and flattened into a vector of dimension pde , which is
then processed by the shared three-layer feed-forward network (identical in structure to Model 1). The shared network outputs one scalar score per team, interpreted as its
predicted performance. The predicted winner is the team
with the higher score.
3. Transformer. The concatenated roster [ c1 ; c2 ] ∈ [V ]2p
is treated as a sequence of 2p tokens and processed using
an encoder-only transformer. Each token is embedded in
Rde , producing a matrix of shape (2p) × de that is fed into
a TransformerEncoder. Positional encodings are omitted
to preserve the order-invariance of the roster set. The encoder outputs are mean-pooled to obtain a de -dimensional
vector, which is passed through a linear layer to produce a
scalar logit; applying a sigmoid activation yields the predicted win probability for team 1.
4. Siamese Transformer. This is an ablated version of the
proposed model, with only the compositional branch to
process [ c1 , c2 ]. This can also be considered a Siamese
version of Model 3.
5. None + Siamese MLP. An ablation of the two-branch design where the composition branch is not active, and the
auxiliary encoder is the same as the proposed model. The
model predicts solely based on auxiliary information.

Implementation Details
All model configurations and parameter choices were determined through empirical tuning, and increasing model complexity did not yield additional performance gains. All models are trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate
of 1 × 10−5 and binary cross-entropy loss, consistent with
the binary (win/loss) prediction task.4

Evaluation Procedure
All datasets are evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation
with 80%/10%/10% train/validation/test splits. Training
stops after 40 epochs without validation improvement, and
the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is used for
testing. Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 are averaged
across folds. The same protocol is applied to all models for
fair comparison.

Results and Discussion
All results are shown in Table 2. This leads to the following
observations and discussions:

Concept Aligned Modeling is Good Modeling
Across benchmarks, the proposed Siamese Transformer attains performance that is comparable to, and often higher
than, the alternative architectures.
This advantage is largely attributed to two architectural
choices. First, the Siamese design encodes each team’s composition independently and symmetrically using shared parameters, encouraging a comparable representation space for
both teams and supporting a fair, structured comparison. The
benefit of this inductive bias is reflected in the consistent
improvements observed when moving from non-Siamese
to Siamese formulations (i.e., Siamese Transformer >
Transformer and Siamese Network > Neural Network).
Second, incorporating a transformer encoder enables withinteam interaction modeling through self-attention, improving
performance relative to a purely feed-forward Siamese baseline (i.e., Siamese Transformer > Siamese Network).
In contrast, the non-Siamese transformer performs worst
and remains near chance across datasets despite having comparable capacity and a similar transformer backbone. The
transformer may entangle two team’s information in ways
that break symmetry and therefore fail to extract meaningful patterns. This suggests that model complexity alone is
insufficient when the architecture does not reflect the target
concept.

Team Composition is Limited but Useful
Without auxiliary information, all models struggle on the
more challenging datasets. This is expected because team
composition alone provides limited predictive signal: the
data drifts over time as rules, strategies, and participant
pools evolve; outcomes also depend on unobserved factors
such as player condition, home advantage, coaching, and
match context; and real-world matchups are often balanced
games, reducing separability from composition alone. These
patterns are especially evident in the LoL Pro and LoL
Amateur datasets where heroes are modified through game
patches, games are highly complex and involve multiple objectives and phases, and the game system will match players
of similar skill ratings.
On the other hand, team composition remains an important contributor to sports outcome prediction. When
auxiliary features are available (as in the LoL Amateur and
Valorant datasets), Siamese Transformer + Siamese MLP >
Siamese Network + Siamese MLP, indicating that self-

attention provides additional benefit for modeling composition. Moreover, both fusion models outperform the variant
(None + Siamese MLP) that discards team composition and
relies on auxiliary information alone. These findings suggest that composition, while limited in isolation, provides
complementary information that improves final predictive
performance when combined with auxiliary features.

Conclusion
The proposed Siamese Transformer demonstrates that meaningful predictive structure can be extracted from team composition, providing a consistent advantage in outcome prediction. This performance gain is attributed to an architecture that encodes each team symmetrically while modeling
within-team interactions via self-attention. Future work may
extend this framework by integrating richer game-related
signals to further improve accuracy and robustness across
domains. This framework opens up future directions in draft
optimization, roster analytics, and game balance analysis.
